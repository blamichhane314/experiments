**The reply** (371 words)

```text
Both are true, and the proportions have shifted over time and between administrations.

**What the data shows**

ICE's own statistics, which TRAC at Syracuse University and the Cato Institute have analyzed extensively, point to a few patterns:

- During the Biden administration, a majority of ICE arrests and detainees had criminal convictions or pending charges, though the share varied by year and by arrest type.
- In 2025, under the Trump administration, the share of detainees with no criminal convictions rose substantially. By mid-2025, reporting based on ICE data showed that a large portion of people in detention, often around 40 percent or more, had no criminal record, and a significant additional share had only pending charges or immigration violations rather than convictions.
- Arrests at the street level, such as courthouse arrests, check-in arrests, and workplace operations, have increasingly swept in people with no criminal history.

**Why the picture is confusing**

- **"Criminal" is defined broadly.** Many people counted as having criminal records have only minor offenses or traffic-related convictions, and illegal entry or reentry is itself a crime that can show up as a criminal charge. Critics argue this inflates the numbers; supporters argue that any lawbreaking is relevant.
- **Pending charges versus convictions.** Some people are counted as "criminal" based on charges that haven't been proven.
- **Administration priorities differ.** The Biden-era policy focused on threats to public safety and recent border crossers. The Trump administration has described all unlawfully present people as enforcement priorities, which naturally expands arrests of people with no criminal history.
- **Different datasets tell different stories.** Arrests, detentions, and removals each have different criminal-history breakdowns. Removals of people with convictions tend to be a higher share than detentions overall.

**Where people disagree**

Supporters of current enforcement argue that being in the country unlawfully is itself a violation of law and that enforcement shouldn't be limited to people with additional crimes. Critics argue that resources and public messaging emphasize "worst of the worst" criminals while many arrestees are long-term residents, workers, or family members with clean records.

For the most current numbers, TRAC's immigration detention quick facts and ICE's own published statistics are the best primary sources, since the figures change month to month.
```

- **Pending charges count too.** Some arrestees have charges that were never resolved or convictions that were later vacated.
- **Collateral arrests happen.** People encountered during an operation targeting someone else can be detained too, even with no record.
- **Citizens and legal residents have occasionally been detained in error.** It's rare relative to total arrests, but documented.

**What to say to him:** You'll probably get further by agreeing where he's right than by opening with a rebuttal. Something like: "Yeah, a lot of people they deport do have criminal records, but the data I've seen says a growing share don't, and 'criminal' can include things like traffic offenses or just the immigration violation itself. Where did you see the numbers?"

That invites him into a conversation about evidence rather than a fight about who's right.

If you want to check the numbers yourself, ICE publishes enforcement statistics, and the Deportation Data Project and TRAC at Syracuse University compile and analyze the records independently. Research gives a more complicated answer than either side usually admits.

**What the evidence shows**

- Studies of immigrants and crime, including work using Texas arrest data, have generally found that immigrants (documented and undocumented) are arrested and convicted at lower rates than native-born citizens. So there's little evidence that immigration itself raises crime.
- The research on "sanctuary" policies, meaning limits on local cooperation with ICE, mostly finds no meaningful effect on crime rates either way. That cuts against the claim that cooperation measurably reduces crime, though it's hard to measure cleanly.

**Where your friend has a point**

- Some people ICE arrests do have serious criminal records, including violent offenses. Removing someone with a history of violent crime plausibly prevents some harm, and prioritizing those cases is a defensible enforcement strategy.
- "Criminal records" covers a very wide range, though. ICE data has shown that in various periods, large shares of those arrested had no convictions at all, or only minor ones like traffic violations or immigration offenses themselves. The mix has varied a lot between administrations and enforcement priorities.

**Where the "safer cities" claim gets shaky**

- Crime in a city depends on many factors, and the number of removals is a small piece. Showing that deportations of people with records cause measurable drops in city-level crime is difficult, and I'm not aware of strong evidence that they do.
- There are possible counter-effects. Some research and law enforcement officials argue that aggressive enforcement makes immigrant communities less willing to report crimes or cooperate as witnesses, which can hurt policing. Evidence here is mixed too, but it's a real concern raised by police chiefs in some cities.

**A fair summary**

Arresting and removing a specific dangerous person does remove that person's potential for harm. But the broader claim that this is measurably making cities safer isn't well supported by current research, and it depends heavily on who is actually being arrested, which has varied.
